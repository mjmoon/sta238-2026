Statistical model for repeated measurements (univariate)

  • The probability distribution \(F\) is the statistical model.
  • We assume that there exists one true distribution.†

†At least until we talk about Bayesian estimation.


A data set consisting of repeated measurements \(x_1\), \(x_2\), …, \(x_n\) of the same quantity is modelled as the realization of a random sample \(X_1\), \(X_2\), …, \(X_n\).

The model may include a partial specification of the probability distribution of each \(X_i\).

Example: A statistical model for the speed of light measurements

What we know/believe …

  • The speed of light in a vacuum is constant.
  • The observed randomness is due to measurement errors.
  • The experiment was carefully designed to avoid any systematic error.
  • The error is likely to be of a large magnitude.

How we may model our knowledge/belief …

  • The observed values are centred around the speed of light in a vacuum.
  • The expectation of the error is 0.
  • The errors have a finite variance.
  • The distribution of the error is symmetric around 0 and tapers off in both ways.

We can model \(\text{Measurement}=\text{True speed}+\text{Error}\), which implies that
\(\mathbb{E}(\text{Measurement})=\text{True speed}\) and \(\text{Var}(\text{Measurement})=\text{Var}(\text{Error})<\infty\).

The Weak Law of Large Numbers

Consider a sequence of independent random variables \(X_1\), \(X_2\), …, with each having the same mean \(\mu<\infty\) and the same variance \(\sigma^2<\infty\). Then for all \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P(|\bar{X}_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)=0,\] where \(\bar{X}_n=\left.\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\right/n\). In other words,

\[\bar{X}_n\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\mu\quad\text{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.\]

Consider a sequence of independent random variables \(X_1\), \(X_2\), …, with each having the same mean \(\mu<\infty\) and the same variance \(\sigma^2<\infty\). Then for all \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P(|\bar{X}_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)=0,\] where \(\bar{X}_n=\left.\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\right/n\). In other words,

\[\bar{X}_n\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\mu\quad\text{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.\]

Proof:

Recall Chebyshevy’s inequality states that

\[P(|Y-\mathbb{E}(Y)|\ge \varepsilon)\le\frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}\text{Var}(Y).\]

for a random variable \(Y\) with \(\mathbb{E}(Y)<\infty\) and \(\text{Var}(Y)<\infty\), and any constant \(\varepsilon>0\).

The Weak Law of Large Numbers

Consider a sequence of independent random variables \(X_1\), \(X_2\), …, with each having the same mean \(\mu<\infty\) and the same variance \(\sigma^2<\infty\). Then for all \(\varepsilon>0\),

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}P(|\bar{X}_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)=0,\] where \(\bar{X}_n=\left.\sum_{i=1}^nX_i\right/n\). In other words,

\[\bar{X}_n\overset{p}{\rightarrow}\mu\quad\text{as}\quad n\rightarrow\infty.\]

  • A random sample is a sequence of independent and identically distributed random variables.

  • The sample mean of a random sample estimates the expectation of the model distribution.

  • The sample mean is an example of a sample statistic.

Example: The true speed of light

The sample mean is a good candidate as the estimate.

  • 299 852 km/s is the sample mean of the observations.
  • 299 792 km/s is the actual speed of light in vacuum.

After adjusting for bending of light in the air, Michelson first estimated 299 940 km/s (1882) then improved the adjustment method to estimate 299 796 km/s.

Actually, the meter is defined as the length of the path light travels in vacuum in \({\frac{1}{299\ 792\ 458}}\) of a second.

Example: Variance of speed of light measurements

The speed of light in a vacuum is now defined as exactly 299 792.458 km/s.

Let \(X_i\overset{i.i.d}{\sim}F_{\theta}\) for \(i=1,\ldots,n\) represent speed of light measurements in km/s where \(\theta=(\mu, \sigma^2)\). \(\mu=299\ 792.458\) is the mean and \(\sigma^2\) is the unknown variance of the measurements.

Suppose we estimate the true variance \(\sigma^2\) with

\[\widetilde{s}_n^2=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\mu)^2.\]

Show that \(\widetilde{s}_n^2\overset{p}{\to}\sigma^2\).

What extra conditions are required on \(X_i\)?

Example: Variance of speed of light measurements

\(X_i\)’s are i.i.d random variables.

\(\implies (X_i-\mu)^2\) are i.i.d r.v.s.

\(\implies\widetilde{s}_n^2\) is a sample mean.

Required conditions for the LLN:

  1. \(\mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_i-\mu\right)^2\right]=\sigma^2<\infty\)
  2. \(\text{Var}\left[\left(X_i-\mu\right)^2\right]<\infty\)

Then, \(\widetilde{s}_n^2\) converges in probability to the mean of \((X_1-\mu)^2\).

\[\begin{split} & \mathbb{E}\left[\left(X_1-\mu\right)^2\right]\\ =& \mathbb{E}\left[X_1^2-2\mu X_1+\mu^2\right] \\ =& \mathbb{E}\left[X_1^2\right]-2\mu \mathbb{E}\left[X_1\right]+\mu^2 \\ =& \mathbb{E}\left[X_1^2\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[X_1\right] & (\mathbb{E}(X_1)=\mu) \\ =& \text{Var}(X_1) & (\text{Var}(X)=\mathbb{E}(X^2) - \left[\mathbb{E}(X)\right]^2\text{ for any r.v. }X)\\ =& \sigma^2 \end{split}\]

\[\implies\widetilde{s}_n^2\overset{p}{\to}\sigma^2\]

Summary

  • Statistical models describe observed data as realizations of unknown data-generating processes using probability distributions.

  • With prior knowledge about the process, we can assumptions to build choose a probability distribution and partially specify a model.

  • The law of large numbers allows us to estimate features of model distributions using sample statistics.
